
I would recommend presenting your **most expensive course first, followed by your mid-priced courses and then your most affordable option**.

This is a pricing strategy known as **price anchoring**, and it connects directly with Robert Cialdini's principles of persuasion in *Influence*.

The objective is not simply to make parents or students spend more. It is to help them understand why your courses are worth their prices.

## **2. Use the decoy effect to guide customers towards your preferred course**

This is another interesting pricing technique.

Suppose you want most parents to enrol their children in your English and Literacy programme rather than basic English conversation.

You could structure the options like this:

| Programme   | Monthly price | What's included                                                      |
| ----------- | ------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Premium     | 650 PLN       | English, literacy, critical thinking and individual progress reports |
| Recommended | 520 PLN       | English, literacy and structured communication                       |
| Essential   | 450 PLN       | English conversation                                                 |

The Essential option is only slightly cheaper than the Recommended option, but offers fewer benefits.

Consequently, the Recommended option may appear to offer the best value.

This is a form of the decoy effect when the pricing and features are deliberately structured to make one option more attractive.

Be careful: the lower-priced option must still provide a worthwhile educational experience.
